# EU AI Act Article 12 — Requirement Mapping

**Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 ("EU AI Act"). High-risk obligations, including Article 12, become enforceable 2 August 2026.** Non-compliance carries penalties of up to €15,000,000 or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

This page maps each Article 12 record-keeping obligation to the specific Sasana mechanism that satisfies it, and to the command that produces independently verifiable evidence. It is a reference for compliance, legal, and security teams evaluating whether Sasana meets their record-keeping duty.

> This is engineering documentation, not legal advice. Whether a given AI system is "high-risk," and whether these mechanisms satisfy an obligation in a specific deployment, is a determination for your own counsel.

---

## The obligation in one line

> "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system." — Article 12(1)

Two words carry the weight: **automatic** (the system records on its own — manual documentation does not satisfy this) and **over the lifetime** (from deployment to decommissioning, across every version). Sasana is built around both.

## Clause-by-clause mapping

| Article 12 requirement | What it demands | How Sasana satisfies it | Evidence artifact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **12(1)** — Automatic recording of events over the system lifetime | Logs generated by the system itself, continuously | The OpenClaw bridge / observer captures every lifecycle event (`SESSION_START`, `LLM_CALL`, `LLM_RESPONSE`, `TOOL_CALL`, `TOOL_RESULT`, `TOOL_ERROR`, `SESSION_END`) at the runtime boundary, with no operator step | `requirement_checks.input_events_logged`, `.output_events_logged`, `.session_lifecycle_logged` |
| **12(2)(a)** — Record events relevant to identifying risk situations or substantial modifications | Capture the conditions that can produce a risk | Tool errors, dropped events (`LOG_DROP`), and model/tool invocations are all recorded; the verifier surfaces `PARTIAL` / `COMPROMISED` states | `requirement_checks.error_events_logged`, `.no_log_drops` |
| **12(2)(b)** — Facilitate post-market monitoring (Art. 72) | Records usable for ongoing oversight after deployment | Every session is a self-contained, replayable JSONL ledger; the evidence pack renders it into a human-readable audit report | `sasana evidence-pack --standard eu_ai_act` |
| **12(2)(c)** — Enable deployers to monitor operation | Records the deployer can inspect and trust | Hash-chained events + Ed25519 signing + optional Archeion server seal produce a tamper-evident, independently verifiable record | `sasana verify <session.jsonl>` |
| **Integrity of the record** (implicit; the point of a log) | The record must reflect what actually happened and be resistant to after-the-fact alteration | JCS-canonical SHA-256 hash chain (each event commits to its predecessor); tampering breaks the chain and the verifier reports `COMPROMISED` | `requirement_checks.hash_chain_tamper_evident` |
| **Trustworthy timing** | A log a party can silently backdate does not enable independent verification | RFC 3161 trusted timestamp anchoring binds an external Timestamp Authority's signed statement of *when* into the chain; without it the record is classified `NON_AUTHORITATIVE_EVIDENCE` | `requirement_checks` + RFC 3161 token in `SESSION_START` |
| **Art. 19 — Retention ≥ 6 months** | Automatically generated logs kept for at least six months | Sessions persist as immutable JSONL / SQLite ledgers under operator-controlled retention (default keep-forever) | Operator storage policy |
| **Art. 10(5) — Data minimisation** | Log the event without unnecessarily storing personal data | Only SHA-256 hashes of prompts, responses, tool inputs/outputs are recorded — never raw content | `privacy_note` in every evidence pack |

## Evidence classes: what "compliant" actually means

Sasana does not claim a binary "compliant" stamp. It classifies the *strength* of the evidence, so an auditor knows exactly what a record can and cannot prove:

| Evidence class | What it proves | Article 12 posture |
|---|---|---|
| `AUTHORITATIVE_EVIDENCE` | Hash chain intact, Ed25519-signed, **and** server-sealed by an independent authority (Archeion) | Strongest — independently attested |
| `SIGNED_NON_AUTHORITATIVE_EVIDENCE` | Hash chain intact and signed, but no independent seal | Operator-attested integrity |
| `NON_AUTHORITATIVE_EVIDENCE` | Hash chain intact, but timing/authority rests with the operator | Proves *not modified after writing*; cannot prove *when* |
| `PARTIAL_EVIDENCE` | Chain intact but with recorded `LOG_DROP` gaps | Incomplete — gaps are disclosed, not hidden |
| `NO_EVIDENCE` | Chain broken / tampering detected | Fails integrity |

The load-bearing distinction is between operator-attested and independently-attested records. A hash chain proves a log was not altered after it was written; only an external anchor (RFC 3161, or an Archeion seal) proves *when* it was written. See [`article-rfc3161-timestamps.md`](./article-rfc3161-timestamps.md) for the full demonstration.

## Producing an audit-ready record

```bash
# 1. Verify the session's integrity and evidence class
sasana verify ~/.openclaw/sasana/<session>.jsonl

# 2. Generate the Article 12 evidence pack (HTML report + JSON manifest)
sasana evidence-pack ~/.openclaw/sasana/<session>.jsonl \
    --standard eu_ai_act \
    --system-name "ClaimsTriage-AI" \
    --system-version "2.1.0" \
    --out ./compliance_reports/
```

The `.html` is the auditor-facing report (system under audit, requirement checks, interaction timeline, root hash, privacy note). The `.json` is a machine-readable manifest anyone can re-verify independently — no Sasana tooling required for the cryptographic checks.

## What Sasana does not claim

- It does not determine whether your system is high-risk under Annex III — that is a legal classification.
- It does not store raw inputs/outputs, so it cannot reproduce content — by design (Art. 10(5)).
- In-process verification checks the RFC 3161 MessageImprint; full TSA signature-chain validation uses standard `openssl ts -verify` against the TSA's published root.
